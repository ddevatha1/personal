A Four-Site Plantar-Pressure Insole: Transducer Error Budget, Feature Equivariance, and Sparse-Versus-Dense Agreement in Overground Walking

Arjun Subramanian, Deeptanshu Devatha, Hrudhai Lothumalla, Arnav Katewale, Leilani Gilpin

Author affiliations listed in the paper: AIEA Lab, UC Santa Cruz (Prof. Leilani Gilpin); MIT CSAIL (first author)

Approach

Dense in-shoe pressure arrays are expensive and mostly stay in the lab. X-Step uses four force-sensitive resistors, at the first, second and fifth metatarsal heads and the heel. The paper deliberately evaluates it as a measurement instrument rather than a clinical classifier. Before any classification score could mean anything, three questions had to be answered: how accurately the sensors recover force, what a four-point sample of a continuous pressure field keeps and loses, and which downstream features survive the errors the device itself makes.

The instrument

  • Sensing: 4 × FSR402 in 10 kΩ dividers on an ESP32's 12-bit ADC, sampled at 25 Hz.
  • Radio: a 28-byte Bluetooth Low Energy frame per foot with a monotonic sequence counter, so packet loss is measured rather than assumed.
  • Calibration: 480 bench observations across 12 loads from 0 to 30 N. In-sample error is 1.69 N MAE and 2.95 N RMSE, with a 2.02 N load–unload hysteresis.
  • Host path: a rolling 4 s window (100 samples) over both feet. Feature extraction and inference average 0.23 ms per update on the development machine, against a 40 ms sample period.

Feature map

Each window becomes 59 named features. Per site, these are peak pressure, mean pressure, coefficient of variation and the pressure–time integral. Per window, they are cadence, stance ratio, anterior pressure share, aggregate peak and total pressure–time integral. The features feed a logistic-regression head with 540 parameters.

The paper shows analytically that these features react differently to sensor error. Threshold-occupancy features are sensitive to a constant sensor offset, while zero-mean noise of the same size does much less damage. Clipped per-window quantile re-baselining removes any constant additive offset before features are computed.

Sparse versus dense

How much of a pressure field can four points capture? To test this, the study used an independent public archive: 149 recordings from 15 healthy adults, made with a 32-cell instrumented insole. For each of X-Step's four sites, the nearest single cell was compared with its surrounding dense cluster.

The side-to-side failure is geometric, not a matter of sensor accuracy. With only these four sites, the estimated centre of pressure is a weighted average of points that barely span the foot's width, so no amount of transducer accuracy can recover mediolateral motion. The 298 foot-takes come from only 15 people, so these correlations are descriptive and no p-values are claimed.

Grouped evaluation

The design choices were explored on a synthetic four-sensor cohort: 24 virtual subjects, 9 overload-pattern classes, 2,592 windows. With subject-grouped evaluation, the logistic head reached macro-F1 0.885 (bootstrap interval 0.861–0.907), against 0.040 for a majority-class baseline. Without grouping it reached 0.931. That 0.046 gap is the cost of transferring to an unseen wearer, and it is larger than the whole spread between the best and worst classifiers tested. Peak-only features scored about the same as all 59 (0.886 vs 0.885), which says the synthetic labels are close to peak-separable. It does not show that the full feature map is needed.

Limitations

  • No human walking data from the X-Step device itself exist yet. The findings are engineering-level and establish neither ulcer prediction nor clinical benefit.
  • The sparse-versus-dense comparison uses someone else's insole, so it characterizes four-point sampling of a pressure field in general, not X-Step specifically.
  • The fault analysis is applied to synthetic pressures. Moving toward any patient-facing use requires a prospective walking study under ethics approval.

My part

I built the machine-learning component: the 59-feature pressure map and the logistic-regression model. I also designed the sparse-versus-dense study above, which asks whether four sites can stand in for a dense array. The first author is Arjun Subramanian. The hardware, firmware and other analyses were team work.
