KidCode: from print() to spaCy in three years

KidCode is a Python program for kids in Frisco, Texas. I wrote the curriculum and teach the classes. Year 1 covers the basics, Year 2 adds files, errors, classes and tests, and Year 3 is natural-language processing with spaCy. This page shows what exists for each year and how solid each part is. The GitHub repository holds the documents, data and code behind every number here.

How to read this

Not all of KidCode is documented equally. There are three kinds of support, and each claim below says which it has.

Two things to know first. The 1,214 programs are normalized, anonymized versions of student submissions: comments were removed, a standard header was added, programs were edited to run, and personal details were removed. The originals are not published. And no dated record from 2024 or 2025 has been located, so those years rest on my account, the curricula and the programs organized under them. The repository's limitations document spells out everything the evidence cannot show.

2024 (my account: first class June 5, 2024). Python from zero: output and variables, strings, decisions, loops, lists, dictionaries and functions, ending in a text-adventure capstone. The Year 1 curriculum, its six project documents and 138 programs are in the repository; nothing from 2024 carries a date.

2025 (my account: began June 5, 2025). assert tests, ciphers and character codes, files, JSON, error handling, classes and inheritance, libraries, and text analysis. Seven project documents and 504 programs; nothing dated.

2026 (my account: year began June 5, 2026). spaCy tokens, stop words, lemmas, sentences, parts of speech, named entities, Matcher patterns and a rule-based chatbot; 572 programs. The dated record of 2026 covers a separate, beginner summer bootcamp, below. No dated record of the Year 3 spaCy classes exists.

The curriculum names three places where a later project returns to an earlier one: Mad Libs → Auto Mad Libs with spaCy (projects 1 → 16), the trivia quiz → a JSON-backed quiz with error handling (5 → 9), and Year 2's text-cleaning code tested against spaCy's tokenizer (project 14). The 2026 record

The summer 2026 bootcamp is the part of KidCode documented as it happened: a new group of families, online (Google Meet and Zoom) with one in-person introductory meeting, teaching beginner Python. Its records agree with each other. The Session 1 summary PDF was created at 11:28 PM on June 28 and posted to the parents' chat at 11:32 PM. The Day 2 summary PDF was created at 2:13 PM on July 5 and posted at 2:18 PM. The Day 2, 3 and 4 slide decks were last saved on June 29, July 10 and July 12, shortly before each class. The full dated table is in the 2026 record.

The chat also shows the ordinary problems: a session cut short by Zoom, one rescheduled and one cancelled for low turnout, and one cancelled because no one from the team was free to teach. Families often communicated by message rather than joining calls, so the chat can't be used as a headcount, and attendance isn't recorded. In the chat I wrote 66 of the 100 messages sent by participants; two other team members and 29 parent messages make up the rest. The chat is private and is not published; dated excerpts without names, numbers or links are in the repository.

Student programs

All 1,214 programs are public, in the repository, one file per student per project, in random order within each project. They are normalized, anonymized versions of student submissions, published with guardian permission. Every one is a working program and every one is textually different, and a script recounts every folder. What the files can and can't show:

  • They show that 1,214 runnable programs matching the 20 curriculum projects exist, with the counts above, and that the constructs in them follow the curriculum year by year (functions in 13% of 2024 programs, 100% of 2025 and 95% of 2026; classes in 0%, 47% and 24%; assert tests in 0%, 48% and 0%; spaCy in 100% of 2026).
  • They don't show when a program was written, how much help a student had, or what a student could write unaided. Because of the normalization, programs the private manifest attributes to one student show no more shared style than randomly grouped programs, so authorship can't be checked from the code. Only working programs were collected, so the corpus can't show how many students tried a project and didn't finish.
  • The 2026 technique counts (of 572 programs): spaCy 572, named entities 203, Matcher 129, lemmas 106, part-of-speech tags 102, sentence segmentation 49, dependency parsing 0. See data/nlp_techniques.csv.

How the number is counted and how the files were prepared: docs/project-corpus.md. Run python3 scripts/reproduce.py in the repository to recount them.

Assessment

Each unit has a pre-test and a post-test written from the curriculum: 13 pairs across the three years (instruments, data and analysis script in the repository). The two forms cover the same skills in the same formats, using different code. For the 104 students with both scores, the mean went from 41.7% to 58.8%, a change of +17.1 percentage points (95% interval 15.3 to 18.9). 99 students improved, 4 stayed the same and 1 declined. By starting cohort, the change was +17.7 (2024), +18.9 (2025) and +14.8 (2026).

This is the observed change in scores. There was no comparison group, so it does not estimate what the classes caused.

Also, per-unit scores and completed answer sheets were not kept: the 104-student percentages came to the repository as a table, and reproduce from it (analyze.py) but cannot be re-derived from completed tests. No administration dates were recorded, and the instrument PDFs were typeset in September 2026. Methodology: assessment/methodology.md.

What this doesn't show

  • Anything dated from 2024 or 2025, including the June 5, 2024 start. The earliest dated public writing about KidCode is June 2026; the June 27, 2026 post describes the summer bootcamp's first gathering, for its new families.
  • How many classes were actually delivered: 120 is the curriculum's length.
  • Who wrote each program, or when. The Year 3 projects have 52 to 118 published programs each (118 for one project), far more than the dated 2026 record suggests (a parents' group of about thirty join events, and sessions where only a few joined). The Year 3 classes are not among the documented sessions, and I can't reconcile the two from the evidence.
  • Any cause for the score changes.
  • Anything from someone other than me beyond system timestamps and parents' own messages in the chat. No testimonials, teacher or community statements have been collected.

Full list: limitations document. Claim-by-claim: docs/evidence.md. My role, with what is dated and what is my account: docs/leadership.md.
