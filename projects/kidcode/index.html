KidCode · Founder & Lead Instructor · 2024–present

Teaching Python to kids, from print() to NLP

I started KidCode in 2024 to teach programming to students in my community in Frisco, Texas. What began as introductory Python classes grew into a three-year curriculum: Python fundamentals, then more advanced programming, then introductory natural-language processing with spaCy.

How it grew

Three years, three curricula

Each year had 40 class-days over eight weeks and ended in a capstone. Each picked up where the last left off, and some projects came back a year later at a harder level.

2024Year 1

Python from zero

KidCode began with students who had never programmed. Year 1 covered output and variables, strings, decisions, loops, lists, dictionaries and functions, and was built around six projects, from a Mad Libs generator to a text-adventure capstone called Escape the Haunted School. Thirty-five students joined that first year, from four neighborhoods.

2025Year 2

Building bigger programs

Year 2 was about writing programs that last. Students learned to test functions with assert, work with files and JSON, handle errors, and organize code into classes and inheritance. The projects grew with it: a cipher lab, a password vault, an upgraded trivia quiz, a virtual pet, a monster battle arena, turtle art, and a text-analysis capstone. Fifty-three new students joined that year, and the program reached seven neighborhoods.

Part of a student's Virtual Pet Care, Project 10: a Pet class with methods for feeding and playing, and a pet that is saved to a file so it is still there tomorrow.

2026Year 3

Natural-language processing

In Year 3, students used spaCy to work with real text: tokens, stop words, lemmas, sentences, parts of speech, named entities, and rule-based matching with Matcher. They built a tokenizer showdown, a keyword detective, a redaction tool, a rule-based chatbot called KidBot, and a capstone each student proposed. Thirty-six new students joined, and the program reached ten neighborhoods.

Inside the classroom

Summer 2026: a bootcamp for a new group of families

In June 2026 I ran an online bootcamp for a new group of families: a first meeting at a neighborhood pavilion, then weekly sessions on Google Meet and Zoom. I wrote the lessons and slides, sent a recap and homework after each class, and later added weekly quizzes, small projects and a points leaderboard. Two other members of the team helped teach.

The classes did not always go to plan. Zoom cut one session short and I moved it to Meet halfway through; on another day only a few students joined, so I moved that class. I wrote about what worked and what didn't in the class posts: When I tried to teach too much in one hour, What happens when you slow down, and The bug that became the best part of class.

Student work

What the programs produce

More than 1,200 student programs are published in the archive, one folder per project. Here are a few of the outputs: a turtle drawing, and charts from the Year 2 and Year 3 text projects.

The curriculum builds on itself

Some projects come back a year later at a higher level. The Year 1 Mad Libs returns in Year 3 as Auto Mad Libs, where spaCy picks the blanks. The Year 1 trivia quiz returns in Year 2 with JSON storage and error handling. And Year 3 opens by testing the text-cleaning code students wrote in Year 2 against spaCy's tokenizer, on sentences like “Dr. Lee lives in the U.S.A.”

The code changes with the curriculum

Across the published programs, functions go from 13% of Year 1 programs to all of Year 2. Classes appear in 47% of Year 2 programs, file reading and writing in 76%, and assert tests in 48%. Every Year 3 program uses spaCy.

What students learned

Before and after each unit

Every unit has a pre-test and a post-test I wrote from the curriculum: 13 pairs across the three years. The two forms cover the same skills in the same format, with different code. Students take them on paper, without a computer.

Of the 104 students who took both, the average score went from 41.7% to 58.8%, a gain of 17.1 percentage points. 99 improved, 4 stayed the same and 1 went down.

Notes & context

  • The assessment shows how scores changed, before and after. There was no comparison group, so it doesn't measure what caused the change.
  • The student programs in the archive were cleaned up for publication: I removed comments and personal details, added a standard header, and edited each program so that it runs. The originals aren't published. The programs were shared with families' permission and carry no names.
  • 120 class-days is the length of the curriculum (three years of 40). Program counts are counts of published programs, not attendance, and I don't have complete attendance records.
  • The 2024 and 2025 class posts on this site were written in September 2026, looking back at each class through the curriculum and the students' programs. The 2026 bootcamp posts were written right after each class.
  • The ten neighborhoods are where students live. KidCode isn't run with or sponsored by any of them.
