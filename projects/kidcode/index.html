KidCode · Founder & Lead Instructor · 2024–present

Teaching Python to kids, from print() to NLP

I started KidCode in 2024 to teach programming to students in my community in Frisco, Texas. What began as introductory Python classes grew into a three-year curriculum: Python fundamentals, then more advanced programming, then introductory natural-language processing with spaCy.

The people behind KidCode

I didn't run it alone

I started KidCode and wrote its curriculum, lessons and slides. As it grew, I recruited peers to help. Friends and fellow students helped me teach classes, organize sessions and expand the program across the community.

In the summer 2026 bootcamp, two of them helped teach alongside me while I ran the schedule, the slides and the weekly quizzes. The program reached four neighborhoods in 2024, seven in 2025 and ten in 2026, and it took more than one person to keep up.

The students and the curriculum

Three years, three curricula

Each year had 40 class-days over eight weeks and ended in a capstone. Students worked through hands-on projects, from Python fundamentals to introductory NLP. Each year picked up where the last left off, and some projects came back a year later at a harder level.

2024Year 1

Python from zero

KidCode began with students who had never programmed. Year 1 covered output and variables, strings, decisions, loops, lists, dictionaries and functions, and was built around six projects, from a Mad Libs generator to a text-adventure capstone called Escape the Haunted School. Thirty-five students joined that first year, from four neighborhoods.

2025Year 2

Building bigger programs

Year 2 was about writing programs that last. Students learned to test functions with assert, work with files and JSON, handle errors, and organize code into classes and inheritance. The projects grew with it: a cipher lab, a password vault, an upgraded trivia quiz, a virtual pet, a monster battle arena, turtle art, and a text-analysis capstone. Inside the classroom

Summer 2026: a bootcamp for a new group of families

In June 2026 I ran an online bootcamp for a new group of families: a first meeting at a neighborhood pavilion, then weekly sessions on Google Meet and Zoom. I wrote the lessons and slides, sent a recap and homework after each class, and later added weekly quizzes, small projects and a points leaderboard.

The classes did not always go to plan. Zoom cut one session short and I moved it to Meet halfway through; on another day only a few students joined, so I moved that class. I wrote about what worked and what didn't in the class posts: When I tried to teach too much in one hour, What happens when you slow down, and The bug that became the best part of class.

Student work

What the programs produce

A few of the things students' programs make: a turtle drawing, and charts from the Year 2 and Year 3 text projects.

The curriculum builds on itself

Some projects come back a year later at a higher level. The Year 1 Mad Libs returns in Year 3 as Auto Mad Libs, where spaCy picks the blanks. The Year 1 trivia quiz returns in Year 2 with JSON storage and error handling. And Year 3 opens by testing the text-cleaning code students wrote in Year 2 against spaCy's tokenizer, on sentences like “Dr. Lee lives in the U.S.A.”

From print() to a chatbot

Year 1 programs are short and ask the player a few questions. By Year 2 students are writing classes that save their state to files, and by Year 3 they are building a rule-based chatbot that answers questions using spaCy's entity recognition and pattern matching.

What students learned

Before and after each unit

Every unit has a pre-test and a post-test I wrote from the curriculum: 13 pairs across the three years. The two forms cover the same skills in the same format, with different code. Students take them on paper, without a computer.

Of the 104 students who took both, the average score went from 41.7% to 58.8%, a gain of 17.1 percentage points. 99 improved, 4 stayed the same and 1 went down.
